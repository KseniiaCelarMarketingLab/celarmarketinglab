The Problem With “Knowing Your Target Audience”

Demographics can describe an audience, but they rarely explain decisions. Learn how to build audience understanding around motivations and behaviour.

“Women, 25–34, urban, interested in fashion.”

It looks like an audience definition.

It is actually a description of a population.

The difference matters.

Marketing teams often talk about “knowing the customer” when what they really know is a demographic profile. Age, gender, location, income and interests are useful variables, particularly for media buying.

But they rarely explain why someone chooses one product over another.

01

Demographics are a starting point, not an insight

Imagine two people who fit exactly the same demographic profile.

Both are 29-year-old women living in Barcelona.

One buys premium skincare because she enjoys researching ingredients and treats skincare as a hobby.

The other buys premium skincare because she has sensitive skin and wants to reduce the risk of irritation.

Same demographic.

Different problem.

Different motivation.

Different search behaviour.

Different objections.

Potentially different message.

This is why audience research becomes more useful when it moves from who people are toward what they are trying to accomplish.

02

The better questions are behavioural

A stronger audience profile might ask:

  • What triggers the search?
  • What problem exists before the customer starts looking?
  • What alternatives do they consider?
  • What makes them hesitate?
  • What evidence do they trust?
  • What does “good” look like to them?
  • Who influences the decision?
  • What makes them switch?
  • What would make them decide not to buy?

These questions produce information that can influence positioning, product development, messaging and channel strategy.

A demographic label usually cannot.

03

Motivation is not the same as interest

This distinction is particularly important in digital advertising.

Platforms can tell marketers that someone is “interested in fitness.”

But that could describe someone training for a marathon, someone trying to lose weight, someone recovering from an injury, someone who enjoys watching sports, or someone who simply follows fitness creators.

The same interest category contains different jobs-to-be-done.

Treating it as one audience can create generic communication.

04

Research should combine methods

There is no single perfect research method.

Quantitative research can reveal scale and patterns.

Qualitative interviews can uncover language, motivations and tensions.

Customer reviews can reveal recurring frustrations in customers' own words.

Sales conversations can expose objections.

Search data can reveal questions people ask before purchase.

Support tickets can expose problems the marketing team never sees.

The strategic task is to connect these pieces.

That is also why customer understanding cannot be reduced to a persona document created once a year.

Audiences are not static characters.

They are people making decisions in particular contexts.

05

The danger of fictional personas

A persona can be useful when it represents evidence.

It becomes dangerous when it becomes fiction.

“Sarah, 32, loves yoga, drinks oat milk and values sustainability” may sound human.

But unless those details are based on research, they are simply assumptions dressed up as insight.

A more useful persona might say:

Customers frequently discover the product through recommendations, compare three alternatives before purchase, worry about switching costs and want evidence that implementation will not disrupt their existing workflow.

That is less colourful.

It is far more actionable.

06

The real audience may be defined by a situation

Sometimes the most useful segmentation variable is not demographic at all.

It is the situation in which the customer enters the category.

For example, a software company may discover that customers behave differently depending on whether they are:

  • replacing an existing system
  • starting from scratch
  • responding to rapid growth
  • trying to reduce costs
  • responding to a compliance requirement

These situations create different motivations even when the customers themselves look similar.

07

What should marketers do?

Start with demographics where they are useful.

Then go further.

Map:

1Trigger→2Problem→3Alternatives→4Evaluation→5Objections→6Decision→7Experience

The goal is to understand the decision-making process, not merely describe the person making the decision.

That produces a much stronger foundation for positioning and communication.

The most valuable audience insight is rarely “who buys from us?”

It is:

“Why does this person need us now, what are they comparing us with, and what makes our offer meaningful in that specific situation?”

That is the difference between targeting a demographic and understanding a market.
